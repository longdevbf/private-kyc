Credential Lifecycle Engine Midnight Buildathon · Wave 1
01Title

Private Credential Lifecycle Engine

Issuance, per-verifier unlinkability, revocation and expiry — written in Compact on Midnight. KYC is the demo sitting on top, not the contribution.

The issuer is mocked. The lifecycle is real.

Put the scope caveat on the title slide. A judge who has seen twenty zk-KYC demos is watching for overclaiming — disarming it in the first five seconds buys you the rest of the deck.
02Problem

Ten platforms. Ten copies of your ID.

Every one of them needed one bit — not a document.

And a credential you cannot revoke is worse than no credential at all.

Two ideas, no more. First the disclosure problem, which is well understood. Then the one that isn't: the day-two problem — revocation, expiry, and verifiers who can collude.
03The gap

Where zk-KYC demos stop

prove(age > 18) — solved, repeatedly. Not solved:

Revoke
without publishing a list that leaks exactly who was revoked.
Unlink
stop two verifiers discovering they served the same person.
Expire
enforce a validity window inside the circuit, not in an API wrapper.

This project is that layer.

Be specific rather than dismissive. Predicate proofs genuinely are the easy part — small circuits, well-documented pattern. The lifecycle is where the design decisions bite, and it is the part every demo skips.
04Architecture

One boundary, enforced by the compiler

Private

on the holder's device
  • holder secret
  • birth date · country · tier · expiry
  • blinding factor
  • issuer signature
  • Merkle path

Public

on chain, world-readable
  • issuerKeys
  • activeCredentials (Merkle root)
  • spentNullifiers
  • leafIssuer
  • revocationEpoch

Attribute values never cross. disclose() gates every crossing — and the build fails without it.

The Compact compiler refuses to build if a witness-derived value reaches public state without an explicit disclose(). All 18 sites in this contract carry a comment justifying that specific crossing. This is a machine-checked privacy boundary, not a code-review one.
05The hard part

Same person, two verifiers, no correlation

Real output from the running demo. Alpha asked one question, Beta asked a different one. Each nullifier is H(secret, verifierId, epoch). A shared byte would light up.

Alpha Exchange

saw · age >= 18
no correlation

Beta Lending

saw · kycTier >= 2
bytes in common: 0 of 32 expected by chance: ~0.13 verdict: unlinkable
Take the third design point if there is time — it is the least obvious. Comparing a private timestamp against block time forces you to disclose a bound on it, and the compiler says so. The fix is a public asOf pinned to a window around block time, with every private comparison made against that instead.

None of them provide the lifecycle. That is the gap.

Say "designed to interoperate with", not "integrated with" — these are design affinities, not shipped integrations, and a judge will ask. Our predicate module is deliberately small and pluggable precisely so richer compliance logic can sit on top without touching the core.

AppendixBusiness viability · 60 seconds

Who adopts this, in order of realism

  1. Other Midnight dApps needing gated access — token sales, DAOs, regulated DeFi. They already need "is this user eligible" and already accept on-chain verification. Lowest integration cost, no compliance sign-off.
  2. Regional exchanges — genuine repeated-KYC pain and real cost per re-verification, but they need a real issuer first.
  3. Regulated fintech — largest need, longest sales cycle, hard requirements we do not meet yet.

Why integrate rather than build it. The predicate proof is a weekend. The lifecycle is not — per-verifier nullifier derivation, an accumulator whose revocation semantics actually hold, and the disclosure analysis that keeps attributes off-chain. Our test suite caught a bug where a bare jubjubSchnorrVerify call silently accepted forged signatures. That is what a team rolling their own would hit.

The blocker: the mock issuer is what stands between this and production.

Keep this to sixty seconds — it is 5% of the score and padding it looks worse than being brief. The blocker is a partnership problem, not an engineering one, which is why Wave 3 targets Identus rather than more contract features.
